# The Struggle

Mexico is arguably the first major social uprising of the 20th century pre-dating the Russian revolution by 7 years. The revolution was the result of decades of tensions mounting between Mexico’s wealthy elites and its working class peasantry. In rural southern Mexico agrarian workers were demanding better wages under the leadership of Emiliano Zapata, while in urban areas like Mexico City women like Hermila Galindo, were making the case for women’s suffrage. In northern Mexico five years prior to the Mexican revolution, workers went on strike at the Cananea copper mine for higher wages and better conditions. These events in the south, central, and the northern parts of Mexico were heralding shifting dynamics of the people who earned their daily bread toiling in the fields or doing other forms of maual labor. 

After decades of rule by the elites, the campesinos, middle class and professional workers began calling for reform of their government. Led by Francisco Madero, Emiliano Zapata, and Pancho Villa, they began embracing a new national identity and advocating against foreign control of mining companies and exploitation of the people and the land. The ensuing violence resulted in Porfirio Díaz vacating his seat of power and retiring to Europe. The next decade, Mexico would erupt into violence changing leadership multiple times. In 1917, under the leadership of Venustiano Carranza, Mexico adopted a new Constituttion. The fighting would continue unabated until the mid-1930s when Lazaro Cárdenas's administration brought stability and realized some of the agrarian reforms put forward by Emiliano Zapata. 

## The Agrarian Myth

{% capture chile_origins_text %}
Mexico's revolutionary myth is rooted in its agrarian and Indigenous past. Idealized by Zapatismo and invoked throughout the years as a symbol of resistance to inspire generations of revolutionaries. Murals depicting Zapata are intertwined with images of Aztec and Maya culture. When Zapata was martyred in April of 1919, his image and legend only grew larger. Emiliano Zapata and Che Guevara are two revolutionaries whose legends have been captured in murals painted on walls, their images have been screen pressed on t-shirts, and reproduced in a myriad of ways capturing the imaginations of millions inspired by their resistance to colonial systems.
